# Tibia Anti-Ad Manifesto

_Sorry for the inconvenience, we just want Tibia._

## The Community Deserves Better

Open Twitch, look for Tibia, and see how long it takes to find someone actually playing Tibia. What should be a place to discover streamers, watch hunts and enjoy the game has turned into a mess of OTServ promotions, Pokémon servers and broadcasts that barely have anything to do with Tibia in the first place. Somehow, finding content about the actual game in its own category has become the difficult part.

And it’s not like nobody has noticed. Players have been complaining about this on Reddit and elsewhere for years. There are countless discussions about OTServs taking over the category, promotional streams dominating the listings and people having to block dozens of channels just to find something worth watching. Some have built their own filters, others have given up browsing the category altogether. Yet here we are, still dealing with the same nonsense.

Twitch hosts these broadcasts, and CipSoft owns the game whose name they’re using. Sure, they have different responsibilities, but from a player’s perspective, the result is pretty much the same: neither has managed to make the Tibia category a decent place to find Tibia content. After years of complaints, expecting someone to eventually sort it out doesn’t seem particularly promising.

So instead of waiting around, I decided to do something about it. That’s how Tibia Anti-Ad started. The original idea was simple: build something that could recognise OTServ promotions and unrelated games, filter them out intelligently and let people browse the Tibia category without having to manually block half of Twitch. No endless channel blacklists, no blindly matching random keywords, just a cleaner way to find the content we actually came for.

## And Then There Are the Fansites

While working on the Twitch problem, I started looking more closely at the websites we use every day. And, well, things aren’t exactly looking great there either. Some Tibia fansites have become so cluttered with advertisements, trackers and promotional content that the actual information feels like an inconvenience squeezed between monetisation opportunities. You open a page to check a quest, look something up or use a calculator, and suddenly you’re navigating around things you never asked to see.

The frustrating part is that these websites genuinely matter. The Tibia community has spent decades building an incredible collection of tools, guides, databases, maps and resources. A lot of that work comes from people who love the game and have contributed far more than anyone could reasonably expect. I appreciate that, and this project isn’t about pretending those contributions have no value. But creating something useful doesn’t automatically mean everyone visiting it should become a source of advertising revenue.

The usual argument is that websites cost money to maintain. Of course they do. Hosting isn’t free, development takes time, and keeping a project running requires effort. Nobody is disputing that. What I don’t understand is when those costs became the visitor’s responsibility to cover through advertising. Looking up a creature, checking a market price or reading a guide shouldn’t come with an expectation that I’ll watch ads, accept tracking or contribute to someone’s advertising metrics.

And before this turns into another discussion about whether an ad is intrusive enough to deserve blocking, that’s not really the point. I don’t want to negotiate how much advertising is acceptable on a page. I want to be able to browse without advertising, full stop. Whether an ad is discreet, animated, floating or perfectly integrated into the layout doesn’t change the fact that I didn’t come there to see it.

## Love the Fansites, Not the Ads

There’s a difference between appreciating someone’s work and agreeing with how they choose to monetise it. Tibia Anti-Ad isn’t against fansites, developers or content creators. Quite the opposite. The whole point is to keep the useful parts of these websites accessible while getting rid of the advertising surrounding them. Articles should remain readable, calculators should work, maps should load and navigation shouldn’t break just because an ad was removed. A blocker that destroys the website along with the ads isn’t much of an improvement.

It’s also worth acknowledging that some fansites already manage to provide useful content without filling their pages with advertising. Those projects deserve recognition, not because they’re necessarily better at everything else, but because they show that an ad-free experience isn’t some impossible fantasy. I’d much rather highlight websites that respect visitors’ attention than keep treating advertising as an unavoidable part of the internet.

And no, blocking ads isn’t an attack on creators. Visiting a website doesn’t make me responsible for its business model, just as appreciating a project doesn’t mean I’m obligated to finance it through advertising. People can support community projects voluntarily, contribute to their development, share their work or help in countless other ways. None of that requires turning every visitor into a monetisation opportunity.

## What We’re Actually Building

Tibia Anti-Ad is an independent project focused on making the Tibia community a nicer place to browse. It brings together browser extensions, content-blocking filters, research and classification tools to deal with advertising, tracking and unwanted promotional content across the websites and platforms players use.

On Twitch, the goal is to make finding actual Tibia streams easier by identifying OTServ promotions and unrelated games. But doing that properly takes more than checking whether a title contains a suspicious word. Full titles and tags provide the evidence and need to be considered together. Sometimes the evidence won’t be enough to make a reliable decision, and that’s fine. I’d rather leave an uncertain stream alone than hide legitimate Tibia content because a classifier decided to be stupid. This isn’t about policing what people are allowed to stream. It’s about giving viewers the option to filter what they don’t want to watch.

For fansites, the approach is just as straightforward: investigate how advertising and tracking are implemented, develop effective blocking rules and make sure the websites still work afterwards. That last part matters. Removing an ad is easy if you don’t care what else breaks in the process. Removing it without destroying the layout, disabling features or making the page unusable takes actual testing. That’s the standard this project should aim for.

I also want the research to be transparent. If something has been tested, it should be documented. If a rule is experimental, that should be clear. If a solution doesn’t work reliably yet, there’s no reason to pretend otherwise. The idea is to build something people can inspect, contribute to and actually trust, rather than another collection of filters that nobody knows how to maintain.

## Taking Back Our Experience

At the end of the day, this comes down to a pretty simple conflict of interests. Twitch benefits from engagement, OTServ operators want visibility, and advertising-supported websites want to monetise their traffic. Meanwhile, most players are just trying to watch Tibia, find information or use a tool without dealing with all the extra nonsense. Those priorities don’t always align, and pretending they do hasn’t made the experience any better.

I’m not expecting Twitch, CipSoft or every fansite owner to suddenly agree with this project. They have their own interests, and that’s their business. But their decisions don’t have to determine what the rest of us are willing to put up with. If platforms won’t improve their categories and websites insist on filling their pages with advertising, we can build tools that let players make those choices for themselves.

That’s what Tibia Anti-Ad is about. Not telling everyone how to use the internet, not attacking people for running fansites and certainly not pretending that every problem can be solved with a few blocking rules. It’s about giving players a practical alternative to an experience that has become unnecessarily frustrating, while keeping the content and resources that make the community worth being part of.

Tibia players have been building their own solutions for decades. We’ve created databases, mapped the game, documented mechanics, developed tools and figured out things that weren’t always explained to us. There’s no reason we can’t apply that same energy to fixing the way we experience the community outside the game.

I don’t think wanting to watch Tibia without OTServ promotions, or browse a fansite without advertising, is asking for much. And I definitely don’t think we should have to wait for someone else’s permission to make that happen.

That’s why I started Tibia Anti-Ad.
